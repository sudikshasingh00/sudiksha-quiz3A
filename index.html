What is a lichen?

A lichen is not one living thing but a partnership. A fungus builds the body and holds on to the surface, and an alga or cyanobacterium living inside it makes food from sunlight. Together they can live where neither could alone: on bare rock, old wood and even rusty metal. Most lichens grow only a few millimetres a year, and many are sensitive to air pollution, so a healthy crop of lichens is a good sign of clean air.

Four to look for

  • Pixie cups

    Small grey-green stalks that open into cups, rising from a base of leafy scales. Raindrops landing in the cups splash tiny pieces of lichen out to start new patches.

  • British soldiers

    Pale, branching stalks topped with bright red caps. The red tips are where the fungus makes its spores. Look for them on rotting logs and old fence rails.

  • Leafy lichens

    Flat, lobed layers that look a little like leaves. The edges often lift away from the bark, so you can see the paler underside.

  • Crust lichens

    Patches that look painted on, growing right into rock and bark. You cannot peel them off without taking the surface with them.
